Are Good Generators Good Decision-Makers?

Policy Learning for General Interventions via Retargeted Counterfactual Generation

Raphael C. Kim1,2 Jingsen Zhu1 Ramin Zabih1 Michele Santacatterina2
1Cornell Tech, Cornell University 2Dept. of Biostatistics & Population Health, NYU Grossman School of Medicine
arXiv PDF Code (coming soon) BibTeX

The Answer: not by default.

A generator that looks accurate can still make bad decisions. Retargeting it toward the decisions it supports fixes this.

What?
Optimizing a policy against a generator trained on logged data pulls the policy toward interventions the logs barely cover, where the generator is most optimistic.
Why?
Regret depends on the generator's error where the learned policy acts, not its global error. The gap is a worst-case density ratio that grows as overlap shrinks.
How?
Train a doubly robust, invariant generator (ADIGen), learn a pilot policy from its rollouts, then retarget the generator toward that policy and relearn.

Key messages

  • Video: out of distribution, regret drops from 0.74 to 0.28 at η = 0.002 (conditional vs. retargeted).
  • Cells: the retargeted generator recovers the rescue peak of all 3 active compounds with only 10% of high-dose wells.
  • Theory: retargeting removes the worst-case density ratio from the regret bound.

1The problem: logs are not experiments

World models promise decision-making from logged experience alone: train a generator of what happens if on the data a system already collects, then plan against it. A driving model learns from fleet logs, a manipulation model from teleoperation sessions, a virtual cell from last year's screens. The catch is that logs are produced by a behavior policy with priorities of its own, and that biases them in exactly the two ways that hurt decisions.

  • Confounding: actions are correlated with hidden context. A fleet's drivers brake hard mostly when something is already going wrong, so a conditional model concludes that hard braking goes with crashes. A teleoperator attempts a fast grasp only when the bin is tidy. In our physics logs, strong pushes co-occur with strong gusts. Conditioning on the action learns the logger's habits, not the action's effect.
  • Vanishing coverage: the interesting actions are the rare ones. Evasive steering at speed, aggressive approach angles, high doses of a promising compound — the actions a planner most needs to evaluate are the ones the logger avoided. In our benchmarks they appear in as little as \(\eta = 0.002\) of the logs.

Planning turns these flaws from noise into bias: the optimizer ranks actions by the generator's imagination and takes the argmax, so it actively seeks the sparsely-covered region where the generator is confidently optimistic. A model that looks accurate on held-out logs can still be a bad decision-maker — that is Figure 1, and global error metrics never see it, because they average under the same logging distribution.

2Method: ADIGen-PO

Counterfactual generators, from physical world models to virtual cells, are increasingly used to choose interventions: simulate rollouts under candidate actions, then pick the best. We address three problems with this, one per step.

  • Biased logs → doubly robust loss. ADIGen (Automatic, Debiased, Invariant Generation) reweights with a universal Riesz representer learned by Riesz regression, so no density ratio is inverted, even for high-dimensional joint interventions.
  • Shifting environments → invariance penalty on learned representations \(S(\bX), T(\bA)\).
  • Generator not built for decisions → retargeting. Learn a pilot policy \(\tilde\pi\), retrain the generator at \(\pi_\lambda = \lambda\tilde\pi + (1-\lambda)\pi_b\), and relearn the policy.
\[ \underbrace{\alpha(\bA,\bX,E)\big[\ell(\theta;Y,\bA)-\zeta_\psi(\theta;\bX,\bA)\big]}_{\text{reweighted residual}} + \underbrace{\int \zeta_\psi(\theta;\bX,\ba)\,\nu(d\ba\mid\bX)}_{\text{plug-in over target law }\nu} + \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}\,\hat{\mathcal I}_n(\theta) \]

Policies are KL-regularized tilts of the logger, \(\pi_\beta \propto \pi_b\, e^{\hat\mu/\beta}\), fit from generator rollouts. In round 1 ADIGen is trained at a fixed intervention law \(\nu_0\). In round 2 it is retrained at \(\nu = \pi_\lambda\), with cross-fitting across data halves.

3Why retargeting helps

The key quantity is the transfer factor \(\rho(\pi\,\|\,\nu) = \|\pi/\nu\|_\infty\): how much a generator's error under its training law \(\nu\) inflates under the policy \(\pi\) that acts.

  • ADIGen's excess risk has a doubly robust product remainder: errors in the weights and in the pilot generator multiply (Thm. 1).
  • Regret follows error under the learned policy, \(\Reg \lesssim \tfrac1\beta\, \rho(\hat\pi\,\|\,\nu)\cdot\text{(generator error under }\nu)\). A conditional generator (\(\nu = \pi_b\)) pays the worst-case \(\rho(\hat\pi\,\|\,\pi_b)\) (Thm. 2, Cor. 2.1).
  • Retargeting caps the transfer factor at the price of an average (\(\chi^2\)) weight cost. The gain grows like \(\sqrt\rho\) as overlap falls, and mixtures are within 2× of the best target law (Thms. 3–4).

4Cell Painting (RxRx19a)

Outcomes are microscopy images of SARS-CoV-2-infected cells. The intervention is compound × dose (256 arms), and the utility is a rescue score from 0 (infected) to 1 (mock). We bias the logs by keeping only a fraction \(\eta\) of VERO wells at doses ≥ 1 µM, which is exactly where rescue happens.

5Video world model (PhyWorld)

Outcomes are rendered videos from PhyWorld, and the generator is a flow-matching DiT in VAE latent space. Each ball gets a push and a shared gust; the policy chooses a second push to land each ball on a goal. Strong gusts (\(|g| \in [0.50, 0.85]\)) lie outside the training range (\(|g| \le 0.25\)) and appear in the logs with probability \(\eta\).
